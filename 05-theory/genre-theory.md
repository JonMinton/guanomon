## 1. The novum and cognitive estrangement (Suvin)

**Darko Suvin** defines science fiction as *"the literature of cognitive
estrangement."* Its necessary and sufficient conditions are the **interaction of
estrangement and cognition**, and its main formal device is *"an imaginative
framework alternative to the author's empirical environment."* *(High confidence —
Suvin's own 1972/1979 essay "Estrangement and Cognition".)*

- **Estrangement** fuses the Russian Formalist *ostranenie* (defamiliarisation) with
  Brecht's *Verfremdungseffekt* — making the familiar strange so it can be seen
  freshly.
- **Cognition** is the rational, scientific/historical register that keeps the
  strangeness *legible* rather than merely marvellous.

The genre-defining engine is the **novum** (Latin "new thing," borrowed from Ernst
Bloch): a *"totalizing phenomenon or relationship deviating from the author's and
addressee's norm of reality"* — the "strange newness" that anchors and organises the
whole fictional world and generates the narrative by estranging the reader from the
present. *(High confidence.)*

> **For this project:** dragon guano-as-fuel **is** a textbook novum — a single
> rule-governed innovation that, once introduced, *totalises*: it reorganises the
> economy, politics, class, magic and ecology of the whole world. That totalising
> quality is exactly what Suvin means, and it is why the story reads as SF wearing
> fantasy's clothes. It is also why the [[critique-and-direction|determinism
> critique]] bites: a *totalising* novum tends toward fable unless something resists
> its logic.

**Correction logged** (the research killed two plausible-sounding claims): Suvin did
**not** coin "novum" in a 1972 *College English* essay, and cognitive estrangement is
**not** simply "alternative realities contradicting the status quo" — it specifically
requires the cognition/estrangement *interaction*. Don't repeat those framings.

## 2. SF vs fantasy — and where the line genuinely blurs

Suvin's classic distinction is **cognitive**: SF *"takes off from a fiction (literary)
hypothesis and develops it with totalizing (scientific) rigor,"* whereas fantasy is
*"a genre committed to the interposition of **anti-cognitive laws** into the empirical
environment."* *(High confidence — primary text.)*

But the boundary is contested, and Suvin himself softened it:

- Suvin later conceded the cognitive / non-cognitive divide *"does not run between SF
  and fantasy but **inside each**."* His own "scientific"/"literary" terms were in
  scare-quotes — analogical, not literal.
- **Istvan Csicsery-Ronay Jr.** (*The Seven Beauties of Science Fiction*, 2008)
  extends and complicates Suvin: a genuine novum *"changes **values** as well as
  conditions"* (rejecting any requirement that it be "necessarily progressive"), and
  what really marks SF is that *"the sublime and the grotesque are inexorably
  associated with science and technology. In fantasy, that's not necessary."* He
  classifies blended works (e.g. Miéville's *Perdido Street Station*) as leaning
  fantasy while conceding individual elements are science-fictional — i.e. authors
  *slide between the two.* *(High confidence — Rain Taxi interview.)*
- **Refuted claim** (don't use): that in the Suvinian frame the novum *"must be
  consistent with current scientific knowledge."* The research killed this — Suvin's
  "cognition" is a critical *method/register*, not a fact-check against real science.

> **For this project:** the guano novum *"changes values as well as conditions"* in
> exactly Csicsery-Ronay's sense — dragon-slayers become villains, hoarded gold
> becomes irrelevant, a woman becomes a unit of measurement. That value-inversion is
> the strongest evidence the piece is doing real novum-work, not set-dressing.

## 3. Sanderson's Laws and the hard/soft magic spectrum

**Sanderson's First Law** (verbatim): *"An author's ability to solve conflict with
magic is **DIRECTLY PROPORTIONAL** to how well the reader understands said magic."*
Magic sits on a **soft-to-hard spectrum**, not a binary. *(High confidence —
Sanderson's own blog, c.2007.)*

- **Hard magic** = explicit, legible rules; the reader knows what a character can and
  can't do (his example: Spider-Man). Legibility is *precisely what licenses* using
  magic to solve narrative problems strategically and satisfyingly.
- **Soft magic** = rules kept deliberately obscure to preserve **wonder/mystery**
  (his example: Tolkien's wizards — "you rarely understand the capabilities of
  Wizards"). Good writers *"very, very rarely"* use soft magic to solve problems,
  because doing so reads as a cheat (deus ex machina).

(The Second and Third Laws — *limitations > powers*, and *expand what you already have
before adding new* — are corollaries; the First Law is the load-bearing one here.)

## 4. The bridge thesis: hard magic ≈ the novum

**This is the synthesis the project rests on — and it is an *argument to be made*, not
a consensus to be cited.** *(Medium confidence — assembled by juxtaposing Suvin and
Sanderson; no verified source states the identity outright.)*

The structural parallel is genuine and tight:

| | Suvin's **novum** | Sanderson's **hard magic** |
|---|---|---|
| Legible? | Yes — cognition keeps it rule-governed | Yes — rules explicitly described |
| Enables problem-solving? | Yes — drives plot rationally | Yes — that's the First Law |
| Totalising? | Yes — reorganises the world | Often (e.g. Mistborn economies) |

So **hard magic functions structurally like the novum**, which makes the **hard/soft
axis a bridge between fantasy and SF**: a sufficiently *hard* magic is, formally, an
SF novum dressed in fantasy costume.

**The honest complication (record this — it's productive, not fatal):** Suvin requires
the novum's legibility to come from a **scientific / cognitive-historical register**,
whereas hard magic's rules can be internally consistent yet **scientifically
arbitrary**. So hard magic satisfies *Sanderson's* "cognition of rules" but not
necessarily *Suvin's* "cognition as science." That gap is not a flaw — it is exactly
**Csicsery-Ronay's blurred-boundary territory**, and it is where *The Guano Guild*
lives.

> **Why this matters for the design:** Guano is the *hardest* possible magic — it is
> not magic at all, it's **chemistry/biology**. It satisfies *both* Sanderson's
> legibility **and** Suvin's scientific-cognition criterion. The story then sets this
> maximally-hard novum against [[vesser|the mages' soft magic]] and dramatises the
> hard system *out-competing* the soft one. The genre argument **is** the plot: the
> industrial novum displaces the artisanal marvel. The hard/soft magic distinction
> isn't just theory behind the story — it's the story's *engine*.
