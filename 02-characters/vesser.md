# Vesser — the displaced mage

**Account genre:** elegy
**Register:** elegiac, dignified-in-defeat, looking back at a world already gone.

## Role in the system
The mage who exhibits motivated resistance to the discovery — at first denial, then
a doomed attempt to out-produce guano in open trials, then capitulation. He is the
**handloom weaver watching the power loom arrive**: not wrong about his own craft,
just rendered uneconomic by it.

## Arc
Denial → competition → exhaustion → capitulation → reinvention as an alchemist
trying to reproduce guano. The reinvention is the most *pathetic* position of all —
he goes from denying the new thing's superiority to trying to counterfeit it.

## What he sees
- The death of a craft, and of the self that craft made possible. He sees this
  clearly and mourns it well.
- Dignity: his defeat is genuine, and he carries it without whining.

## What he's structurally blind to
- **That he was never the centre.** The elegy assumes a lost golden age in which
  magecraft mattered; the determinist reading is that magecraft was *always* the
  artisanal bottleneck waiting to be industrialised. His grief is real but his
  cosmology is self-flattering.

## Key beats
- The trials — for Grundrak this is triumph; for Vesser the same scene is the
  beginning of the end. Same external event, opposite arc position. This is the
  Rashomon mechanism working at its cleanest, so write the *trials* twice and make
  the two chapters share concrete details while diverging completely in meaning.
- The alchemists' workshop — his coda, the incumbent who reinvents the disruptor and
  shelves it.

## Voice notes
- Lyrical, backward-looking, fond of the *texture* of vanished labour.
- Avoid self-pity; the dignity is what makes the displacement land.

## Open questions
- Is Vesser the same mage present at the discovery, or a representative figure? (The
  chat split discovery and trials — decide whether Vesser anchors both.)
- Does he ever glimpse the self-flattery in his own elegy, or is the irony entirely
  the reader's?
