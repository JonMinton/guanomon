# Critique and direction — why Rashomon

## The diagnosis

An external critique (the other-Claude evaluation linked from the blog) identified
the story's defining flaw: it is **technologically determinist**, and determinism
is structurally **anti-story**, because it decentres the individual agency that
narrative conventionally requires. The guano wins regardless of what anyone wants
or does. There is no contingency, so there is no drama — only the unrolling of a
foregone conclusion. It reads as fable.

The earlier-draft symptom: one narrator per scene, and every narrator agrees about
both *what happened* and *what it meant*. No epistemic friction anywhere. The
middle sags because nothing is at stake interpretively.

## The move

Rashomon does **not** require abandoning the determinist argument — that argument is
the point of the piece, and softening it would be a different, worse story. Instead
it relocates the conflict:

- **Out of** the question *"what happens?"* (settled: the technology wins)
- **Into** the question *"what does it mean, and to whom?"*

The same events, retold from different subject positions, become different stories
with different *genres*. Determinism at the system level; radical disagreement at
the level of interpretation. The reader assembles the truth from accounts that
cannot all be flattering to their narrators and cannot all be reconciled.

### A deepening of the thesis (the dragons question)
Determinism here must **not** be read narrowly as "the techno-optimist story wins and
keeps winning." Its fuller form: **a system that misprices its own foundation is
*determined* to fail.** The civilisation builds everything on a base of living,
intelligent agents (the [[dragons]]) it treats as inert resource — so the eventual
fragility/collapse is *also* deterministic, the unpriced externality coming due. This
matters because it lets the story take its **chosen** ending — the dragons coordinate and
defect (see [[dragons]] option C) — **without** betraying the determinist thesis: the dragons don't *choose*
freedom heroically; the suppressed variable reasserts itself mechanically. The iron law
simply turns on the winners. Framed this way, the bleakest ending is the *most*
determinist, not the least — which is exactly what keeps it from sliding into
[[plot-beats|Boxer/Animal-Farm]] uplift.

## What each account contributes

This is the engine of the rewrite. Each POV is a different *genre* reading of one
deterministic process:

| Account   | Genre                        | What it sees                          | Structurally blind to        |
|-----------|------------------------------|---------------------------------------|------------------------------|
| Grundrak  | political thriller           | class vindication, the system's logic | the hollowness of his win    |
| Vesser    | elegy                        | the death of a craft and a self       | that he was never the centre |
| Brask     | the maker's monologue        | the Hum as a beautiful solved problem | what the Hum is *for*        |
| Dalla     | satire of commemoration      | her own erasure-by-celebration        | any route out that keeps status |
| The King  | horror                       | his obsolescence, in real time        | nothing — and that's his curse |
| The Princess | conversion / bildungsroman | the new order as home, not as loss  | that there was anything to mourn |
| Dern      | war confession               | his reduction to remote butcher       | that the heroism was always a lie |
| The Guild | corporate annual report      | a clean progress narrative            | every human cost above       |
| The Dragon| undecided / fable-from-outside | the cage that comfort built          | the analogy to Dalla         |

*(Dalla also splits into two accounts — on-air persona and private monologue; see
[[plot-beats]]. The cast is deliberately growing; audit for redundancy as it does.)*

The Guild account is the keystone irony: it is the *only* narrator that resolves
all the conflicts — by erasing them. Its tidiness is the indictment.

## Two design principles to hold to

1. **The reader knows more than any narrator.** Every chapter should leave a gap the
   reader can fill from another chapter. The King and Grundrak should be readable
   *against* each other; Dalla and the Dragon should rhyme without either knowing
   the other exists.
2. **Genre is characterisation.** A character's account *is* the genre they live
   inside. Don't describe Vesser as elegiac — write his chapter as an elegy and let
   the reader infer the man.

## Scaffolding

Dan Harmon's **Story Circle** remains the per-chapter unit (you/need/go/search/
find/take/return/change). Each account can run its own small circle over the shared
events, so that the *same* external beat lands at a different position in each
narrator's arc. That mismatch — climax for one narrator is glue for another — is
where Rashomon gets its texture.
