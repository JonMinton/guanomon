# The Guilds: State of the Kingdom Address

---

> *Being the Annual Address to the Confederated Guilds, delivered by the Chair of the Sappers' Guild on the occasion of the founding, as hummed across the provinces at the appointed hour.*

It has been another year of progress, and it is right that we pause to measure it.

It falls to the Sappers' Guild to give the address this year, as it has these several years past, and we are aware of what that custom signifies. The age runs on what we lay and dry and press and tend — the feedpipes threaded through the rock, the drying-floors, the compression-works, the patient daily management of force. It is only natural that the hand on the apparatus should be the hand that holds the gavel. We say this without vanity. A foundation does not boast; it bears the weight, and asks only to be recognised for bearing it. The other guilds are gathered here under that recognition, and we are glad to give each its due.

A generation ago this was a poorer, darker, and more dangerous world. Let the record show the distance travelled. Where once the night was lit only by those who could afford to waste tallow, the meanest household now draws light and warmth at a price that falls each year. Where once a journey across the city consumed a morning, it is accomplished, below, in comfort, in minutes. The figures are set out in the appended tables and they admit of only one reading. Adoption is universal. Output rises. The cost of energy, expressed in the standard unit, has never been lower. A grateful age may be forgiven for no longer remarking on marvels it has come to expect as its due.

Founding is a thing best remembered plainly. The discovery that transformed our fortunes is sometimes told as a matter of chance; we prefer the truer telling. A discovery is not the accident that precedes it but the *understanding* that follows, and the understanding was the guilds': the assay, the trials, the patient characterisation of the substance and its properties, the long disciplined work by which a curiosity became an industry. We honour, of course, the individual with whose name the standard unit is associated; her place in the public affection is secure and well deserved. But sentiment should not crowd out fact. What was found that day was a possibility. What was built in the years since was a civilisation. The guilds built it.

The transition of the Magicians' Guild deserves particular notice, as a model of managed change. Where a lesser order might have resisted the future, that ancient profession was guided, with our support, into new and productive channels — first into alchemy and the careful study of the fuel, and latterly into the development of the communication apparatus by which this very address reaches you. That latter work has now grown great enough to be given a charter of its own: we welcome the Hummers' Guild among us this year, the youngest of our number, sprung from the oldest. No tradition was lost. Every tradition was *repurposed*. We count this among our proudest achievements: that the old skills were not discarded but invited, gently, to become useful.

The Beekeepers' Guild, within living memory a quaint fellowship of a few rural hands, is now among the largest in the confederation, for the honey of the footbee has proved indispensable to the work, and the sector that supplies it has brought employment to many. Work near the apiaries carries hazard, as all vigorous trade does; this has been true since the first man tamed fire, and the provisions are kept under continuous review. Such incidents are, in the proper context, exceptional. They are not, and should not be made into, the story.

The Slayers' Guild, too, has been guided wisely into the age. Its ancient art has been turned outward, to the lands across the water that have not yet learned what we learned; the projection of our methods there has been efficient and, by every measure that can be kept, sparing of our own. And with prudent foresight a portion of that art has been brought home, in the new fellowship of Peacekeepers, who see to it that the settlement of authority — now orderly and complete — remains settled. Order is maintained. The numbers are not in dispute.

On the matter of public safety, the confederation speaks with confidence and with figures. It is the plain and verifiable truth that fewer of our people die by violence today than in any year our grandfathers knew. The old wars — the contested, ruinous wars of succession and border and pride that once emptied a generation of its young men — have simply ceased. We say to any who would trouble the public mind with anxious talk: consult the total. The total has never been kinder.

It would be incomplete not to acknowledge that an economy of this scale generates, as all honest labour does, its share of incident. Work near energy carries hazard; the Sappers' Guild knows this better than any, for it is our own people on the drying-floors and at the compression-works, and we do not speak of it lightly. The provisions are kept under continuous review. These costs are real, they are managed, and they are, in the proper context, exceptional. They are not the story. The story is the light in the window and the warmth in the hearth and the journey made in minutes, and the long peace, and the falling total.

It would be ungracious, too, to pass over the trades that feed the foundation itself. The husbandry of the source consumes meat in quantities our grandfathers could not have pictured, and so the **Farmers'** and **Butchers'** Guilds — modest fellowships in the old world, concerned with the village market and the feast-day — have grown into quiet pillars of the modern economy, raising, slaughtering, and delivering livestock to the lairs on a schedule as dependable as the tides. Whole herds, optimised and uncomplaining, are turned by way of the source into the very light in your windows; it is a marvel of management that the public, enjoying the light, need never once picture the herd. We salute these guilds for their discretion as much as their diligence. The arrangement is efficient, it is humane by every standard the relevant committees have set, and it is, like a good deal else, best not dwelt upon at the table.

As to the foundation of it all, the confederation is pleased to report that the source remains well cared for. The herd is larger than at any time in our history and continues to grow; the animals are housed, fed, and tended at considerable expense, their conditions superior in every respect to anything nature afforded them. Their care is so complete that even the incidental products of their wellbeing find a grateful market — the **Confectioners' Guild** reports record demand for its finest delicacies — a tidy demonstration that nothing in this economy need ever go to waste. Observers report them docile and content; their keepers describe them, often, as *singing*. We find no cause for concern in any quarter, and every cause for confidence. A foundation this broad, this well-managed, and this thoroughly understood does not fail.

We end where we began, with measurement, because measurement is this Guild's first principle and its final defence. Sentiment can be argued. Memory can be coloured. But a figure in a column is a figure in a column, and ours are good, and they improve, and they will continue to improve, world without end.

It has been another year of progress.

It is right that we pause to measure it.

We have measured it.

The age is sound.
