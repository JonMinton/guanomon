# Marga

---

You want to know what it's actually like, so I'll tell you, and it won't take long, because it isn't much.

It's heavy and it stinks and it's the same every day. That's the whole of it. People want there to be more. There isn't more. You go down, you shovel, you fill the bags, the line takes them up, you come home and you can't get the smell out of your hair and you eat and you sleep and you go down again. Twenty years. The knot of it parts under the blade in the same way every time; your arms learn it and then the rest of you goes off somewhere while your arms get on. That's the only mercy in it, that your arms will do it without you. I've shovelled whole afternoons I don't remember. I expect I'll shovel a few more.

So. You want a day. Take today; it'll do for any of them.

The good torches go down first, lowered on a line, and the light finds the gold before it finds the floor. It always finds the gold. The hoard throws it back in a hundred colours — coin and plate and the long teeth of fallen crowns, one king's ransom drifted over another like snow that's learned to gleam. A century ago men died on that slope for a single armful of it. They came with songs and good steel and most of them stayed, and the ones who climbed out climbed out rich or not at all. Kael doesn't look at it. "Another day in paradise," he says, and goes down the ladder backwards, the way you go down when your knees have opinions.

The smell comes up to meet you before the floor does. It isn't the smell the stories promise — no brimstone, no sulphur of the pit. It's warm and animal and faintly sweet, the sweetness of something kept too long in the dark, and it sits at the back of the throat and stays there through supper.

I come down last, because I'm the one who shovels, and the shoveller wants the others already clear when she reaches the muck.

She fills the far third of the chamber like weather. In the bad light she's less a beast than a landscape — a long ridge of flank rising and falling, slow as a tide, each breath a sound you feel in the wet floor through your boots. There's new pipe up her near side in bright lengths, bracketed to the rock, fatter than last season's; somebody's been reinforcing the cradle again. She doesn't stir. We've learned the schedule of her and she, perhaps, the schedule of us. The visits come less often now. That's meant to be a mercy. It only means more to lift each time.

Aldric keeps his eyes on her and his back to the work, which is the old habit and the useless one. He's old enough to have come down here for the other reason, in the other years, with a blade instead of a blade-shaped shovel. He watches her breathe the way you watch a thing you were trained your whole youth to fear and are now forbidden to touch. *Forbidden* is the wrong word and the right feeling. You do not raise a hand to the herd. You do not foul the source. You go to your corner of the floor and you keep to the wet and you keep, above all, clean. He raided, in his day — real treasure, real risk, the songs and all of it. Now he holds my torch and watches a sleeping mountain breathe and lets his knees take the blame for the shovel he doesn't lift. I let him. Somebody has to remember it was ever grand. It's not me. I was born to the muck. I never knew it any other way and I don't grieve a thing I never had.

The wet is the safe part. The fresh stuff is soft and sodden, near black, and the torches stand in it without complaint; you could drop a flame in it and lose the flame. It's only later, only dried and aged and elsewhere, that it becomes the thing the whole world now runs on. Down here it's just muck, and the rule of muck is the first rule they teach you: it doesn't burn, it doesn't blow, it ruins your boots and your marriage and nothing else. Shovel the wet. Bag it. Send it up. Stay out of the dry.

So I work. The knot of it squelches and parts under the blade and I lift and turn and the bag takes it, and my arms have done this so many thousand times that the counting part of me has gone somewhere quieter and better. A crown surfaces under my shovel — a real one, gold to the eye, a band a man was once anointed in — and I lever it aside without a thought, the way you'd lever a stone out of a furrow, and it rolls back into the glittering drift and is gone.

"Mind the treasure," says Kael, who's holding the bag and grinning. It's the oldest joke we have. Nobody minds the treasure. The treasure's the one thing in this kingdom that no longer needs a guard, because there's not a soul left alive who'd climb down a dragon's throat for *gold* — gold, when the real fortune is the stuff she leaves behind her, free for the lifting, if you can stand the smell and the back and the strangeness of stepping over a hoard to get to a turd.

We fill the bags. The line takes them up, one by one, into the better air. The dragon breathes. The torches burn low and safe in the cold black muck, and the gold lies where it's always lain, untouched, unwatched, throwing the last of the light back in a hundred colours, to nobody at all.

That's the day. That's just a Tuesday. I don't know why anyone would make a story of it.

There's a woman they named the unit after. The energy. So-and-so many of *her* to light your house. She's on the Hum all the time, lovely voice, very funny, tells how she fell in it the once and it started the whole world. Fell in it the once. *[I have been in it every day for twenty years and nobody's named so much as a bad smell after me.]* I don't hate her. You can't hate weather. She fell in it one afternoon and got a hat and a voice that goes into every house, and I didn't fall, I just stayed, and the difference between us is one wet afternoon thirty years ago and nothing else at all, and that's not unfair exactly, it's just *how it went*, and there's no use I ever found in turning it over. She got the once and the hat. I got the twenty years. Neither of us got asked.

My husband died of a sting. I'll say it the once because it belongs in any honest account of the work and then I'll leave it, the way you leave it. A sting. He worked the other side, the sweet side, the bees — and one got him, and that was that, quick, the way they go. People who don't do the work look at you when you say it, waiting for more, for the grief to start performing itself. There isn't a performance. He went out well and came home dead and I buried him and went down the next morning because the bags don't fill themselves and the rent doesn't care. That's not me being hard. That's just the size of it. You learn the size of things, down here. Most things are smaller than the people upstairs need them to be, and a few are much, much bigger, and the bee that killed my husband is one of the big ones, and I am not going to make it small by explaining it to you, and I am not going to make it bigger than it sat in my own kitchen, either. He died of a sting. A lot of them do now. Make of it what you can; I've a shift in the morning.

They have grand stories, the others. I hear bits of them, the way you hear thunder in the next valley — a king got thrown off a chair, the mages turned into something else, there's a war over the water that we're winning so hard it doesn't count as a war. It washes over. None of it has my name in it and none of it changes the weight of a full bag, and a full bag is the one fact I can't argue with, so it's the one I keep.

I know what you want me to say. You want me to say that we hold it all up — that it's us, the shovellers, the bag-fillers, the ones in the actual filth, that the whole shining business stands on our backs and that if we ever stopped it would all come down. And maybe that's true. I've even thought it, on the bad mornings. But here's the thing nobody upstairs wants to hear: knowing it doesn't hand you anything. What would I do with it? Stop? And eat what? It's a true thought and a useless one, and I've a cupboard full of true useless thoughts, and they don't fill a bag either. So I leave them up on the shelf with the rest and I go down and I shovel, because that, at the end of all of it, is the part that's real, and the weight of a full bag is the only honest thing anybody in this whole story has handed me.

It's heavy and it stinks and it's the same every day. I told you it wouldn't take long.
