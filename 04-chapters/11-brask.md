# Brask

---

The voice was the easy part. I say it plainly because everyone has it backwards. They treat the carrying of a voice as the marvel — the seventh-bell broadcast, the woman with the hat, the whole warm nightly business — and treat what came after as a footnote to it. It is the reverse. Carrying a voice I solved in a season, more or less by accident, by asking a question the others were too busy grieving to ask. After that it was only engineering, and the engineering was the interesting part, and the engineering is what I should like to talk about, if you will let me, because no one ever does.

A sympathetic resonance is a simple thing. Strike a fork, and a second fork tuned to its pitch will answer it across a room, untouched. We knew it as boys; it was a party trick. The voice-carrying was only that trick made faithful enough to hold speech. But a fork that answers is a fork that has *received an instruction*, if you are willing to see it that way, and almost no one was, because they were all still thinking about *talking*. I was not interested in talking. I wanted to know whether a thing could be made to *do* — to move, to turn, to hold a course — on nothing but a pitch sent across the empty air to it. That is a better question. It took years, and I enjoyed all of them.

To make a thing move on its own it must carry its own force, and force is not my art. I understand resonance; I do not understand fuel, and I have never pretended to, which already sets me apart from most of my guild, who would sooner pretend to understand anything than admit a boundary. The fuel is the dwarves' art. And here I will put down the one thing in this account that is about a person and not a problem, and I put it down because it is *true*, not because it is affecting: I could not have done any of it without Korrin.

Korrin is a fuel engineer, and the only collaborator I have ever had who did not waste my time. We are alike, I am told — I do not know enough about dispositions to judge it, but I will allow that we want the same thing, which is for the work to be good, and that we are both indifferent to nearly everything else other men appear to find important. He does not mind that I am a mage and I do not mind that he is a dwarf; we found, with some relief on both sides, that neither of us had ever once understood why a person was supposed to care about that. He brought me the dial — the way the same dried muck will yield a long slow heat or a single blow, entirely according to how it is packed and pressed — and I brought him the fork, and between us we asked the question that mattered: *what if the blow could be steered?*

I will spare you very little of this, because it is the best part. The first difficulty is weight. A singing-engine small enough to ride in the breast of a flying thing has almost no voice; its answer to the controlling fork is faint, and faintness is fatal, because a device that mishears its instruction by a hair at the launch will miss by a field at the distance. So you must either make the engine louder, which makes it heavier, which demands more fuel, which makes it heavier again — a spiral every engineer knows and hates — or you must make the *listening* finer, so that a faint clear pitch suffices. We chose listening. Korrin tuned the burn so steady that the device's own running no longer blurred its hearing; I cut the engine down and down until it was little more than a single sympathetic reed; and we married the two, and the thing held its course clean across a yard on a pitch I could have hummed under my breath. Korrin looked at it and said it was clean. It was. It was the cleanest thing I have ever built.

After that the problems multiply, pleasantly. One device is a curiosity; the difficulty and the beauty is in *many*. You cannot steer a hundred by hand, so you set them on a graded ladder of pitches and let a few controlling forks govern the whole flight — and the flight holds together not because any one hand directs each device, but because each is listening to the same few tones, and to the devices nearest it on the ladder. Out of that, with almost no one directing it, a great coordinated motion comes. I think it the most satisfying result of my life, and I have never been able to make a single person care about it. They only want to know what it is *for*.

What it is for is not my department, and I find the question faintly tedious, but I will answer it, since it is the one thing anyone ever asks. They tell me it will change how the lands across the water are managed, and how order is kept at home, and I expect they are right; Korrin's people and the new fellowships have plans for it I have not troubled to follow. Someone asked me once, idly, over the work, whether one could be made large enough — or made to swarm thick enough — to bring down something *truly* large. They meant the source, I believe. The great beasts in the deep mountains. Should one ever turn troublesome, they said, or need correcting. I told them yes, of course; it is only a question of mass and number; the principle does not care in the least how large the target is. They seemed unsettled by how quickly I answered. I never understood that. It was an easy question. The hard questions are all in the listening.

I came up under Vesser. People are sometimes surprised by that, because they think of him as he is now — a name on the better invitations, a man who can fill a hall by consenting to stand in it, the great genial face of everything the Hum has *done for us all* — and they think of me, when they think of me at all, as something that took place in a back room. But when I was young he had the bench and the standing and I had neither, and I will say plainly that he gave me my start. He simply could never keep still long enough to use his own. He was not a details man. A problem would be three moves from solved and he would be gone — to a gathering, a delegation come to be charmed, some question of who must be told what and in what order. He loved the *meaning* of the work far more than the work, and he was extraordinarily good at the meaning; I am told he is half the reason the country took the Hum to its heart as fast as it did — that he made it feel like a wonder rather than a wire. Perhaps. You cannot solve *fashionable*; it was never a problem that held me. So while he was out making the thing beloved, I stayed at the bench and made the next thing, and then the thing after that, and he never quite noticed that the student he had handed his spare problems to had walked off with the whole future under his arm. I bear him no grudge for it. He gave me a quiet bench and went away to be adored, and I had the better end of that bargain by a distance neither of us has ever measured the same way.

For the record, since this is meant to be a record: the voice-carrying was mine, and so is the steering. You will not find my name on either in anything they hum to the provinces. The Guild prefers to say the understanding was *the guilds'*; Vesser's name comes up, and the woman's, and it is all correct enough in its fashion and I do not contest it. A name is not a problem. I have never once managed to make myself mind. I did the work. The work does not ask that anyone know I did it. It asks only to be done well, and it was.

They are calling the small ones hummingbirds now, which is a sentimental word for a clean piece of work, but I have stopped minding what people call things. The name is not the problem. The next problem is the problem. Korrin thinks we can halve the weight again, and I think he is right; I think that, if we are careful, we will build a thing that hears one voice across the whole width of the kingdom and goes, unerring, to the single place it is told. I should like to see it done before I die. It would be very clean.

That is the work. I am told an account like this is supposed to have more people in it. There were never very many people in it, for me. There was the fork, and there was the fuel, and there was Korrin, who understood, and there was the listening — which is the only thing I have ever loved, and which is about to become the loudest thing in the world.
