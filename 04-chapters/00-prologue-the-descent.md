# Scene 1 — The Descent

*Unattributed prologue. Present tense.*

---

The good torches go down first, lowered on a line, and the light finds the gold before it finds the floor.

It always finds the gold. The hoard throws it back in a hundred colours — coin and plate and the long teeth of fallen crowns, one king's ransom drifted over another like snow that has learned to gleam. A century ago men died on this slope for a single armful of it. They came with songs and good steel and most of them stayed, and the ones who climbed out climbed out rich or not at all.

Kael does not look at it. "Another day in paradise," he says, and goes down the ladder backwards, the way you go down when your knees have opinions.

The smell comes up to meet them before the floor does. It is not the smell the stories promise — no brimstone, no sulphur of the pit. It is warm and animal and faintly sweet, the sweetness of something kept too long in the dark, and it sits at the back of the throat and stays there through supper.

She comes down last, because she is the one who shovels, and the shoveller wants the others already clear when she reaches the muck.

The dragon fills the far third of the chamber like weather. In the bad light she is less a beast than a landscape — a long ridge of flank rising and falling, slow as a tide, each breath a sound you feel in the wet floor through your boots. New pipe runs up her near side in bright lengths, bracketed to the rock, fatter than last season's; someone has been reinforcing the cradle again. She does not stir. They have learned the schedule of her and she, perhaps, the schedule of them. The visits come less often now. That is meant to be a mercy. It only means more to lift each time.

Aldric keeps his eyes on her and his back to the work, which is the old habit and the useless one. He is old enough to have come down here for the other reason, in the other years, with a blade instead of a blade-shaped shovel. He watches her breathe the way you watch a thing you were trained your whole youth to fear and are now forbidden to touch. *Forbidden* is the wrong word and the right feeling. You do not raise a hand to the herd. You do not foul the source. You go to your corner of the floor and you keep to the wet and you keep, above all, clean.

The wet is the safe part. The fresh stuff is soft and sodden, near black, and the torches stand in it without complaint; you could drop a flame in it and lose the flame. It is only later, only dried and aged and elsewhere, that it becomes the thing the whole world now runs on. Down here it is just muck, and the rule of muck is the first rule they teach you: it does not burn, it does not blow, it ruins your boots and your marriage and nothing else. Shovel the wet. Bag it. Send it up. Stay out of the dry.

She works. The knot of it squelches and parts under the blade and she lifts and turns and the bag takes it, and her arms have done this so many thousand times that the counting part of her has gone somewhere quieter and better. A crown surfaces under her shovel — a real one, gold to the eye, a band a man was once anointed in — and she levers it aside without a thought, the way you'd lever a stone out of a furrow, and it rolls back into the glittering drift and is gone.

"Mind the treasure," says Kael, who is holding the bag and grinning. It is the oldest joke they have. Nobody minds the treasure. The treasure is the one thing in this kingdom that no longer needs a guard, because there is not a soul left alive who would climb down a dragon's throat for *gold* — gold, when the real fortune is the stuff she leaves behind her, free for the lifting, if you can stand the smell and the back and the strangeness of stepping over a hoard to get to a turd.

They fill the bags. The line takes them up, one by one, into the better air. The dragon breathes. The torches burn low and safe in the cold black muck.

And the gold lies where it has always lain, untouched, unwatched, throwing the last of the light back in a hundred colours, to nobody at all.
