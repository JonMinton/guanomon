# Grundrak

---

> *Set down in his own hand, begun in the second year of the keeping; one leaf added later. Found among his working papers.*

Measure a thing and it stops lying to you. That's my whole trade, and my whole crime, depending who you ask, and I stopped minding which a long time back.

I'm setting this down early, you should know. The stuff is still a curiosity on my bench, not yet the wage that lights every house and gets charged back to it. Before the chair. Before the towns go bright of an evening. The afternoon the old world ended has already happened. The old world just hasn't been told. Seems I'm the one telling it.

Start with what I am, because the people upstairs spent three hundred years arranging not to look at it. I'm a dwarf. Five feet and a bit standing, near five across, four front to back. A brick. They meant it for an insult. I kept it. A brick is honest — you know what a brick is for. You stack enough of them and you get the wall, the wall holds the floor, the floor holds the hall, and the people up in the hall never once think about the wall. That was us. The ore, the stone, the props, the pumps, the deep kept dry, year on year on year, while the men up top sang about swords and the mages up top sold them tricks. Nobody ever wrote a song about the wall. I didn't make that grievance up. I got it the way you get a trade, off the people before me. Difference is, I put mine to work. They just sang.

The assay, then. You hand me a thing and I don't ask what it's for, or whose it is, or what the songs say about it. I ask what's in it. I ask what it does — under load, under heat, in quantity. That's all an assay is. The mages asked what a thing *meant*. I ask what it *does*. Get up close to an "arcane art" with a scale and a flame and most of the time it's a plain trick with a rule round it saying nobody else is allowed to learn the trick. Take the rule off and you've got an ordinary man doing an ordinary job, and a lot of people kneeling for no reason. That's what an assay does to a mage. It makes you enemies. I could carry that.

How it came to me was an accident, and I'll set it down straight, because the tellings that come later will be prettier and they'll be wrong. They'll want a saint in it. A crew came up from the deep, filthy with the stuff nobody had learned to want yet — down for the gold, the way crews went down then, and back up sick of everything that wasn't gold. The woman who led them stripped off by the fire, the way you do when you've been in the deep and can't stand your own clothes another minute. Her clothes went near the flame to dry. The day's muck was dried into the weave of them, and so was the honey, ground in off the bee-side of the work — though it took us a long while to work that part out.

It didn't burn. It blew. Took the fire and a good part of the workshop with it, and it said, in one flat bang, that the filth the whole world had spent three hundred years stepping over to get at the gold was the strongest thing any of us had ever held in a hand. The old valuers had it half right. Gold the treasure, this the filth. They just kept the wrong half.

That's the accident. *Where there's muck there's brass*, the old people said, and they meant it small — a saying for getting by. They didn't know they'd named the floor the whole world stands on. But an accident is only a question shouted the once. I made it ask again. I brought the stuff to the bench and put my questions to it. Wet, it does nothing — drinks your flame, ruins your boots; there's a mercy in that, or the pits would've gone up generations back. Dried, it burns. Dried and met with the catalyst — the honey, common as muck, in everything — it doesn't burn, it answers, all at once. And there's a dial between the two. Pack it and press it one way, you draw a long slow heat to turn a wheel all night. Pack it the other, you get a single blow that takes down a wall. I found the dial. That's not magic. That's measurement. The mages spent three hundred years guarding what they couldn't explain. I explained it in a season, and explaining it is what turned a marvel into an industry.

So I put up the trials, and I put up the terms, and the terms are the part they'll never forgive me, because the terms were fair.

Here's what I gave them. I didn't ask the mages to grovel at some job picked to shame them. I let them name the work. They named the stamps — the big iron heads that lift and drop and crush ore to meal — because the stamps are honest work you can see, and lifting is the oldest thing their art does and the proudest. Good. Lift them, then. We'll count the strikes, and we'll count past the first hour, past the second, into the night and out the far side, and whoever's stamps are still falling at the dawn count has proved the one thing a power is ever asked to prove. Can you do it again. And again. And again. Without rest, without a temper, without a soul in you that gets tired.

Vesser was something in the first hour. I'll say it here and I'll say it anywhere. I've watched a lot of men be good at things and I've hardly ever watched anyone be that good at anything. The heads went up like he was breathing them up, clean and high and ringing, and the clerk's count ran out ahead of mine, and the crowd made the noise crowds make when they think they're watching the old world win. I didn't make that noise. I was watching the curve. I've spent my life watching curves, and I knew the shape of this one before the man making it did. Brilliance has a slope. It's the slope of a thing using itself up.

By the third hour his strikes had a hitch in them — a man's wrist remembering it's a wrist. By the fifth there was sweat standing on him in the cold, and his count had stopped running ahead and started keeping pace, and then it stopped keeping pace. My engine didn't know any of this was going on. It doesn't know it's in a contest. It eats the dried fuel and turns the wheel and the heads come down at the rhythm I set, the same in the dark hour as the bright one, the same when Vesser was a legend and the same when he was a sixty-year-old man in a wet shirt who couldn't lift his arms.

I called the halt myself, before the dawn count. I want that put down. I sent a man over with a stool and a cup of water and I stopped the clock while he sat, and I've been told since that this was the cruelty — that the water was the worst of it, that to be *spared* in front of them all was the thing he couldn't survive. Maybe. I'm a poor judge of what a courtesy costs a proud man. I spent my youth on the receiving end of theirs. We were *graciously permitted*, my people, for a great many years. We were *kindly tolerated* below. I learned what kindness from above is worth, and I won't apologise for handing some back and meaning it, even if it killed him slower than contempt would have.

This is the part the histories get wrong, so I'll set it down plain. It wasn't a triumph. I'd thought it would be. I'd carried the picture of it for years — the floor, the count, the curve crossing, the old order watching its own arithmetic go up on the wall — and when it came it lasted about as long as the count did, and then it started, very quiet, to go out of me. It's been going out of me ever since.

Because here's the thing I didn't assay, and I assay everything. I won the floor so the floor would stop mattering. I broke the mages' hold so that no man's birth or guild or art would decide what his work was worth — only the work, only the curve, only the honest count of the strikes. And the world looked at what I'd done and it didn't stop minding birth and guild and art. It just added a new thing to mind, and put me at the top of it, and called *that* fair.

They'll give me a throne for this, in time. I can feel it coming the way I felt the curve coming. And I'll take it, because the only thing worse than sitting in that chair would be leaving it for one of them. And the day I sit in it I'll have proved, with my whole life, the one thing I most wanted to disprove:

that the chair was real all along, and that even I would climb over the gold to reach it.

So the trials are done, and found — let the histories dress it how they like — for muck over magic. The curve crossed. The old power sat down on a stool and drank its water, and there's nothing left to prove in a yard with stamps and a clock.

What's left is to show the rest of them what the curve means. The neighbours across the water who still burn wood and field their brave straight lines of men. The walls that still think they're arguments. The trials settled what the stuff *is*. Now we settle what it *does* — at distance, in quantity, to anyone who hasn't had it explained yet.

Now onto the demonstrations.
