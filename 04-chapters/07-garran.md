# Account the Seventh — Garran

*War confession. Opens on the confession. Arc-peak: the Hummingbird demonstration.*

---

I killed a dragon once.

I say it first because there is no good place to put it later. I killed a dragon, with a spear and four good men and most of a night, and three of the four men died doing it, and for that killing they gave me a name that meant something in every hall in the country, and I wore the name for twenty years, and now when I say the sentence aloud — *I killed a dragon* — the young ones look at me the way you would look at a man who told you, smiling, that he had once burned a granary in a famine. Boasted of it. *I dynamited the well, in the dry season, for sport.* That is the face they make. And they are right to make it. I have come around to their face.

You do not kill the thing the whole world eats from. I know that now the way I know my own hands. But there was a world — I lived in it, it was not long ago, it was *real* — in which the bravest thing a man could do was the very thing that is now the most obscene, and in which the three men who died beside me died heroes, and not, as they would die today, vandals interfering with the supply. Nobody has moved their graves. But the meaning has moved out from under them, quietly, in the night, the way meaning does now, and left them lying under words that no longer point at anything.

They did not throw us away, the slayers. Give them that. They found us new work. Across the water there are peoples who have not learned what we learned — who still kill their dragons, still burn wood, still field armies of *men*, brave ones, ranked up in the sun with their fathers' courage and their grandfathers' steel. And we are sent to demonstrate to them. That is the word. *Demonstrate.* You bring the fuel, the dried fuel, packed and primed, and you stand off at a distance no spear will ever cross, and you demonstrate, and where their bravest line stood there is afterward a smell and a silence. It is not a battle. A battle is a thing with two sides. This has one side and one weather. I have done it more times than I will set down and I have never once been in danger, and I have come to understand that the absence of danger is not the reward for valour. It is the *end* of valour. Valour was only ever the name we gave to the risk. Take away the risk and what you are doing is simply killing, very safely, a great many people who cannot reach you. There is a young word for it in the ranks now. I will not teach it to you.

Here is a thing that happened that I did not expect, and that I want to be fair about, because being fair about it is the one piece of honesty I have left. When killing stopped being a matter of the body, it stopped mattering who held the spear. We recruited on strength, always, on the strongman's frame, on who could carry the charge and swing the blade; and then there was no blade, there was only the fuel and the aim and the cold arithmetic of where to stand, and a slight clever woman can stand in the right place and read the arithmetic as well as any ox I ever drilled — better, often, having less to prove. So I took them. Women, in the slayers' ranks, selected on the only thing that pays now, which is aptitude. I did the sums and I could not fault the sums; the fuel does not care whose hand sets it. And I drill them well and I am proud of the good ones and I would not have a single one of them gone.

And I lie awake and grieve the very thing their presence proves is dead. I miss the world where it had to be the ox. Where strength was *worth* something, where the body was the weapon and the courage was the man and not the calculation. I administer the new fairness all day with a straight back and I mourn the old unfairness all night, and I know — I am not a stupid man, only a displaced one — I know that the old unfairness was unfair, that it was nothing to be proud of, that I am grieving a hierarchy that wronged half the people now standing in my ranks. I know it. The knowing does not touch the grief. They sit in different rooms in me and they do not speak.

Then they showed me the new thing, and I understood that even my grief was out of date.

They took me to a yard and they let one fly. Small — smaller than a hunting-bird, a bright wicked little nothing of metal and the fuel and one of the singing devices in its breast — and it went up, and it *hung* there, and then a man a quarter-mile off, sitting in a chair, looking at nothing, moved his hands, and the little thing crossed the yard on its own and went into a straw target shaped like a man and the target was not there any more. No one stood up. No one risked anything. The man in the chair did not even watch it strike; he was already looking at the next one.

I have spent my life being made obsolete by degrees, and I thought I had felt the whole of it, and I had not felt this. Because *I* am still in the chain, do you see, I still stand off and aim, I am still, barely, the hand that does the thing. And the little flying thing is the world telling me, kindly, in a yard, on an ordinary afternoon, that the hand is the last part it has not yet replaced, and that it has now started on the hand.

There will be no young slayer who inherits what I was, the way the dwarves have their young ones and the mages theirs. The thing that comes after me is not a person. It is a swarm. It needs no courage and no strength and no name and no grave, and it will do, perfectly, safely, at any distance, the one thing I was ever for.

I walked out of that yard and for no reason I could name I thought of the dragon. The one I killed, twenty years gone. The eye of it, in the dark, when it knew. I have not thought of that eye in years and I do not know why it came to me then, in a yard full of clever men congratulating each other, watching the little bright thing go up again. I put it away. There was nothing to connect it to. I am sure there was nothing to connect it to.
