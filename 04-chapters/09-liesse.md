# Liesse

---

> *Five letters, carried with the grain fleets, from the wife of an officer of the Compact to her mother across the water. What survives is the Censor's fair copy, taken for the Compact files. Passages struck by that office are marked thus: […]*

**The first letter.**

Mother —

They tell me you will have this before the feast of the lamps, if the fleets are kind. I am well. I begin with that because you will read everything after it looking for whether I am well, and I would spare your eyes the search.

You will have heard by now what happened at the river meadow. I was not there. I will not write here what was told to me by those who were, […] and afterwards it was very quiet, they say, for a long time, and the birds came back before anyone moved.

The terms were read out in the square in both tongues. Theirs is the shorter tongue. Everything in it takes fewer words than it deserves. Father stood very straight, and signed, and I am part of the signing — there is a word for it which the interpreter rendered as *surety*, and Father says it is an honour, and that I am the seal upon a great peace. A seal is a thing pressed, Mother. I looked it up in their tongue, to be certain. It is the same word.

I write in their tongue, as we are asked to; the office must be able to read what it carries. Forgive the shape of my sentences. You taught me better ones.

I sail with the third fleet. I am well.

**The second letter.**

We came the last day of the journey under the ground. I will not try to make you believe the speed of it; you would think fear had written the number. There are stairs cut down into the rock, and lamps that no one trims, and a warmth that comes up out of the floor from no fire I could ever find, and then a thing arrives out of the dark with its own wind walking in front of it, and you step inside, and the world is moved past you faster than a hawk falls. My husband watched my face the whole way and was pleased with what my face did. It is a moment they prize here, I have learned — the moment the new wife sees what the carriage can never be again.

The doorways below are cut low, and one bends to pass them. I bend. My husband bends also — he bends all day here, at doorways, and at certain persons — and across the water, you will remember, nothing bent him, and the meadow was very quiet. I had thought it would comfort me to learn that even they must bow to someone. It did not comfort me. It taught me only how long the stair is, and which step we have been given to stand upon.

There is a box in every room that speaks. It can be made quiet. I have tried.

**The third letter.**

You ask how I manage with the tongue. Better than I wished to — that is the truth of it. The box teaches me. It speaks all day, and above all its other voices there is one woman's voice, warm, in every room of every house, a woman they love here for having fallen, once, into the very beginning of everything they have. She tells the story of her fall herself, and laughs first, and the whole country laughs after her. I understood her laugh a month before I understood her words. Laughter was the first thing in this country I could read.

And I have learned more from her than the grammar, Mother. She says true things about herself in a voice that makes them safe, and they applaud her for it, and the truth does her no harm, and no good either. It can be done with grief as well as with falling. I am becoming skilled at it.

We say at home that the river does not argue with the sea. I have not said it aloud here. The rivers here run under the ground, where they were put.

They call me Liss. It is as near as their mouths come to me, and it is kindly meant, and answering to it costs me […]

**The fourth letter.**

Your last letter reached me with the weather in it missing — lines gone, here and there, as though rain had got into the mailbag. I understand their rain better now than I did. Send me nothing, after this, that the weather could damage.

He is kind. I set that down plainly, because you will be afraid that he is not. He is kind the way the warmth here is kind: it is everywhere, it is even, no one is making it, and there is no one to thank and no way to refuse it. The house is warm. The work of my day is light. The box speaks. The doors are low. Nothing in any day of mine is cruel, nothing, and if you stood before me now and took my hands and asked, I do not know what I would tell you. At home this month we would have kept […]. I did not keep it here. The box does not know the songs.

We say at home that the tree that bends keeps its — I have lost the way it goes, Mother. You will know it. It will come to me when I am not looking for it.

The feast of the lamps falls next month, by my counting, which I keep in our calendar still, in my head, where no fair copy is made of anything. Light one lamp for N—, where the others can see it lit, […] and one for me, where they cannot.

**The fifth letter.**

I am with child, Mother. There. You are glad — I can hear you being glad from here, up through the floor of the world.

I find that I hope the child will be at home here. I had not planned that hope. It arrived the way the warmth arrives, out of the floor, from no one. I hope that the child is at home, and speaks the shorter tongue first, and goes through the low doors without ever once feeling the bend, and is happy. I cannot tell whether that hope is my love or their victory. I have looked for the difference, Mother. I cannot find it, and I have stopped believing the child will need it.

When this reaches you, say my name at the hearth. My whole name, the one […]. Say it where the fire can hear it. Here I am Liss, and I answer to it, and lately, Mother, when they call it across the house, it sounds like me.

Your daughter, […]

*[The remainder of the correspondence is routine. — Office of the Censor; filed with the Compact papers in the twenty-sixth year of the keeping.]*
