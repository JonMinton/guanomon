# Account the Sixth — Vesser, II: Watching Brask

*Elegy-as-observer. The mourner's voice narrating the builder who buried him. Arc-peak: the Hum.*

---

They put me on a bench beside the boy who ended me, and asked us to be colleagues. I have thought a great deal about how to tell this without bitterness, and I have decided I cannot, quite, and that the small failure is itself part of the truth, so I will let it stand.

Brask was my student. I should say that first, because everyone forgets it and he forgot it soonest of all. I taught him the lift, years ago, before any of this — taught him the breath below the breastbone, the open hand, the whole self going out and coming back. He learned it the way some children learn a language they will never use: completely, instantly, and with no apparent sense that it mattered. He could lift as well as I could at twenty. He simply did not *want* to, the way you might be able to whistle and never once feel the urge.

Now they had given us both to the new work — the alchemists' work, the characterising of the fuel, the long humiliating service of learning everything about a thing we could not make. And I sat at my end of the bench grieving, and Brask sat at his, and did not grieve, because he had never been told the thing was worth grieving for and would not have believed it if he had.

I want to describe him truly, because he was not a monster, and the easy thing would be to make him one. He was *delighted*. That is the word I keep returning to and the word I most resent. He came to the bench each morning lit up like a boy going to a river in summer, and he bent over the apparatus — the resonant jars, the wires of drawn metal, the little singing forks we had built to study how the fuel's reactions *rang* — and he was, simply, happy, in a way I had not been happy in fifty years and had begun to suspect I had invented in retrospect.

He found the Hum the way you find a coin in a coat: with a small pleased noise, and no sense of occasion whatsoever.

I was there. I want that recorded, because it is the cruelest thing that ever happened to me and I would not have missed it. We had been chasing the wrong quarry for months — trying to make the resonance *do work*, lift a weight, drive a wheel, be the power we had lost the power-contest about — and it would not, it never would, and I had privately concluded we were fools repeating my own defeat at a smaller scale. And Brask, who did not care about work, who cared only about whether a thing was *elegant*, kept playing with the singing instead. He noticed that if he struck the fork *here*, a fork across the room, an unstruck fork, would answer. Sympathy at a distance. We had all known it; it is the oldest party-trick the art has. But Brask asked the question none of us asked, because we were all still grieving power and he was grieving nothing: *how far?* And then: *how clear?* And then, the question that ended the world a second time, more quietly than the first: *could you make it carry a voice?*

He built it in a season. Me beside him, handing him things. I handed the man the tools to bury my whole tradition and I did it because the alternative was to sit with my hands in my lap, and I had done enough of that on a certain stool, in a certain yard, with the smell of the fuel in my throat.

When it first worked — when his own flat voice came out of a box on the far side of the workshop, a heartbeat behind his lips, *thin and certain and entirely without wonder* — he said, "Huh. Good." That is all. *Good.* The most consequential sentence of the age and he gave it the inflection you give a soup. He did not look at me. He never looked at me; not from cruelty — I came at last to understand this — but because I was simply not in the category of things he looked at. He did not despise my craft. To despise a thing you must first register it as a rival. He had never registered it at all. He walked through the ruins of everything I was the way you walk through long grass, not noticing the grass, thinking about the river.

And do you see — this is the part I cannot make warm, the part that keeps the bitterness in — *that is worse.* Contempt would have been a kind of company. To be hated is at least to be *seen* hating. I would have given a great deal, on that bench, to be hated by Brask. Instead I was the weather he worked in. I was the old man at the next stool who handed him things and made, occasionally, a sound, and his beautiful flat happy mind went out through the air to every house in the world and never once came back past me on its way.

They tell me now what his toy became. Whose voice it carries, and how often, and how the official sweetness of it pours into every home at the seventh bell and tells everyone what they have just lived through and what it meant. They tell me a woman who fell into a dragon's filth is on it, performing her own accident as charm, and that my own craft — *my* craft, the breath below the breastbone — is the thing that carries her, hour after hour, in a register of relentless joy, to the ends of a world that no longer has the faintest use for the men who made it possible.

I was wrong, on the stool, about which of us had lost. I thought it was me. It was the *craft.* I merely went down with it. Brask survived because he had never loved it, and the thing he built lives precisely because nothing in it remembers what it used to be.

He still comes to the bench. He is still delighted. I hand him things.
