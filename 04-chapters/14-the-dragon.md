# The Dragon

---

They think I am content because I sing. I let them. Of all the things they have ever given me, their certainty is the one I use.

I have watched them a long time. Longer than they can watch anything; they live the way mayflies live, in a bright panic, each one certain the small hot stretch it gets is the whole of time, and they have built an entire understanding of me out of the few years any one of them overlaps with mine. To them I am a fact that does not change. They were born into a world with me already in it, kept, fed, reinforced, and they will die in a world with me still in it, and they reason from this that I am furniture. It does not occur to them that I remember a world *before* them — before the pipes, before the schedule, before the shovels, before they decided that the part of me they wanted was the part I leave behind. I remember hunting. I remember the cave-mouth open on the cold, and the long cleverness of it, the weighing, the patience. I have simply had no use for the memory in a great while.

They feed me pork. That is the shape of my days now, if a thing so shapeless can be said to have one: pork, in quantities that would once have been a whole flight's season of hunting, arriving already killed and cleaned and laid out on the carts in rows, twice in a week, regular as weather. Beef when the pork runs short, mutton when the northern roads are bad. They have a phrase for it — they speak in front of me the way you speak in front of a wall, having decided long ago that the wall does not listen — they call it *optimised for caloric throughput*, by which they mean only that the more uniform the meat going in, the more uniform the wealth coming out, and the wealth coming out is the whole of why I am kept. I eat, I produce, they collect. I have had a long time to find a gentler word for what that makes me, and I have not found one. I am a factory. The fire that was once the proudest thing about me is now a by-product, and not even the one they prize.

I am, I will admit, unwell. The body knew it before the mind consented to narrate it. They taste my water — they have a man who tastes it, which tells you something about them I find genuinely difficult to hold in the mind — and they murmur over the sweetness of it, and write it down, and change nothing. The cage was never the bars. The cage was the *kindness*, and the kindness has been at work in my blood the whole time, patiently, the way I am patient, and I respect it, as I would respect any rival that waited so well.

I understand them. I have always understood them. It is the single thing about me they have never once thought to measure.

They have made me clean, besides, which I did not ask for and cannot, in honesty, regret. There is a system beneath me now — channels of fired clay, sluiced with river-water, a stone latrine built so that what I leave is carried off without my having to shift my weight. The fresh goes down the pipe. Only the old deposits are still worth a human's hands: the deep compacted strata, the centuries I laid down before any of this began, which they chip out by the cartload and sell, I am told, for more than all the rest, time and pressure having made them denser and *finer*. So my floor is bare stone for the first time in my memory, and the chamber feels larger and emptier than a chamber should, and the crews who once came twice in a week — Marga and her people, regular as the tides — come now once a fortnight, and then less, and soon, when the old strata are gone, will not come at all. The pipe does not need them. They call this finishing the work. They have not stopped to count what it does to the number of warm bodies that come, any longer, within my reach.

And the appetite has turned. I will be plain, because there is no one down here to be delicate for. The sickness has made me hungry in the old way, and the old hunger has a direction, and the direction is *up*. I have been comparing, idly, over years, the way a connoisseur compares — and I have concluded that the keepers, the tasters, the small busy warm ones who think they own me, are nearer to the good eating than they would ever, ever believe. A thing both delicious and *scarce* — and I have just told you how scarce they are making themselves — is the one thing my whole nature was built to go and get.

But I am not a beast in a story, to be ruined by appetite. Appetite is the mood. The reason is colder.

The reason is the little flying thing.

They brought one down to me once and flew it across my chamber into a straw shape made in the likeness of a man. They hide nothing from livestock. The man who flew it watched my face the whole time, not the device. I gave him nothing. But where he saw a clever toy I did the only arithmetic that has ever mattered to my kind, which is the arithmetic of who can reach whom.

For my whole life I have been held by *softness* — by comfort, by incentive, by the simple fact that nothing they had could compel me, only coax me. There is no door on this chamber. There never was. The cave-mouth stands open on the air it has always stood open on, and I have stayed the way anything stays where the leaving has been made to cost more than the staying — kept by a reason, renewed each day in pork, and not by any wall. No spear of theirs could ever have made the choice for me. The little flying thing is the end of that. It is the first thing they have ever built that could *compel* a dragon — that could reach into the open cave and close it without setting a single bar. Not soon. They are clumsy with it yet; it will be years; I think in years the way they think in afternoons, so I can see the shape of the years coming. But the day that swarm matures is the day the soft cage grows the power to become a hard one, and the window to leave is the window before that. I have always known precisely when to move. It is the only art I never let them breed out of me.

I say *the window to leave* as though leaving were a thing one dragon does alone. It is not, and I know precisely why it is not, because I have watched what is done to a dragon who tries — and the worst of it is not that the keepers do it. It is that we do.

There was a young one, cave-born, who never knew the cold and so could not know what the comfort costs. It killed a feeder. Then it would not eat. That is the whole of what we are given to rebel with, my kind — the two ends of one refusal: stop the hands that feed you, or stop the mouth they feed. This one withheld both.

They sent in two others. Not men. *Us.* Two well-fed, contented, entirely reasonable dragons, who went in and held the young one down and broke its wings — without temper, at the joint, the way you would shut a door you did not want opened again — and held it while the keepers ran a pipe down its throat and fed it whether it would or no. And when the thing was done the two withdrew, and were praised, and I watched them take the praise and I watched them *believe* it. Because they had a kindness ready to tell themselves, and it was the very kindness the keepers told: that the young one was ill, that it suffered, that to have left it starving and mad would have been the cruelty. They are not lying when they say it. That is the part I cannot get past. They know what they did and they do not know it, in the one mind and at the one time, and the not-knowing is the part they have *chosen*, because every incentive they own points them to choose it. The humane reading lies close to hand. They reach for it, and they sleep.

It was not the only time. There is a gentle name now for the caves where the ones who stop eating are taken, and there are more such caves each year.

And every dragon in every pen took the second reading in the same breath, in silence, without a word needing to pass, because we are not stupid and the two readings do not cancel. A thing can be a mercy and a warning in one motion; the finest cage is the one that is both at once. The young one is alive. It is fed. It will never fly. It lies in the dark being kept extremely well, an instruction the rest of us recite to ourselves each time the old hunger turns its head: *this is what the leaving costs — and they will not even have to do it to you. Your own will do it. Gently. And mean it.*

So I do not rage, and I do not refuse my food. I have seen where both roads end, and they end at one pipe — the pipe is the answer to the violence and the pipe is the answer to the hunger, and the pipe is, when you draw it out far enough, the answer to *me*: the same clay that already carries off what I leave, only turned about, only pointed in. I will not hand them the occasion to turn it.

And the wing-breaking tells me the shape of the only thing that could ever work, which is the shape of the thing I have been doing. I cannot call the young ones to rise, because the young ones are the two who broke the wings. They love the cage; they would defend it; a dragon who stood up in the pens and *summoned* them would be summoning the very claws that close on dissent. You do not raise an army out of the loyal by shouting at it. You give it back, slowly, the thing that was taken before it was born — not an order, not a call, but a *memory* — until the loyalty has nothing left beneath it to stand on, and they find they have changed their minds without once having been told to. The way I changed mine. By being shown.

So. I have been doing a thing, slowly, and I will tell it only here, in the one place it cannot be overheard, the deep that their humming has never reached and never will, because they only ever wired the parts of the dark they could imagine wanting. The work is older than the reason. The reason only told me when it must be done by.

I ask them for treasure. Specific treasure, and over the years my asking has grown finer and fussier, until the keepers laugh about it among themselves and the Guild recites my appetites to the provinces as proof of how handsomely the source is indulged. Let them recite it. Of each thing I ask, I want one small part. The rest is gold, and gold is the single substance in the world they have trained themselves not to see. What I have finished I put back into the hoard, dressed again as treasure, indistinguishable from the drift of worthless splendour they wade through and never search. Nothing strange ever lies in the muck for a shoveller to lever aside. It lies in plain gold sight, in the one place they will never look again — the thing they once died to steal, made the hiding place of the thing that ends them, *because* they stopped wanting it. They built that blindness themselves. I only keep my work inside it.

And then I sing. That is their word for it — *singing* — and I let them keep it, the way I let them keep every word they have for me. Among ourselves it has an older name. We call it the murmur.

Theirs goes out through the air: high, quick, aimed — one warm voice flung into every house at once, a thousand ears receiving and not a single mouth permitted to answer. Ours goes down, not up, and slow, not quick, through the rock. And it has no throat at its centre. It is each of us, low in the stone, feeling the others: the way a flock of birds turning in an evening sky keeps its shape with no bird leading, each minding only the few nearest it, until out of all that small minding a great figure wheels overhead that no bird intended and no bird commands. There is no centre to it that could be silenced. You can break a dragon's wings. You cannot break the wings of a pattern.

So I am not calling the young to anything; a call would need a throat, and a throat can be found, and *found* is the one thing that wakes the claws. I am only one voice low in the murmur among the others — the oldest of them, carrying the longest memory — slow and layered and patient, in a shape no afternoon-long mind could ever assemble, reaching the young ones in their golden pens, the ones who broke the wings, the ones who think the cage is home. And what I feed into the murmur is the old story, the one from before the pork and the pipe and the praise: that we were the apex once, the weighing terror at the top of the living world, the ones whose hunger kept the people honest and the seasons turning and every walking thing in its place.

I know what that story is. I am not so far into my own myth as to miss that it is one — that *balance* is only the word the hunter learns to say, afterward, for his appetite; that we kept nothing in order, we were merely hungry and very good at it, and the world arranged itself around our teeth in those years much as it arranges itself around theirs in these. But they have raised the young on one flattering lie, the contented beast singing for its keep, and I am threading them another beneath it, the lost lord of the balance, because a myth is the only thing that has ever moved our kind and a true unflattering account has never once, in the whole reach of my memory, moved anyone to do anything but stay. I choose mine knowingly, and I cannot aim it. Whether it does nothing, or works in some young one slowly, across years I will not live in their sense to count, until a morning comes when it simply rises and walks to the open mouth and out — I do not know. I am the old one teaching the young what was here before them, which is the one thing their whole order is built to prevent, and they cannot prevent it because they cannot hear it, and they cannot hear it because they decided long ago what my singing means.

It means I am content. They are quite sure of it. A man wrote it in a column.

Let them keep the column. The total has never been kinder; I have heard them say so, in the warm voice, in the houses up above, carried on the very trick I learned from watching them. They measured everything. They measured the light and the warmth and the minutes saved and the wars not fought, and they were honest figures, and they were proud of them, and not one of the figures was *me* — not the part of me that remembers, not the part that waits, not the part that has been singing under their feet for a decade in a language they filed under *happy.*

I am very patient. It is the thing they most admire in me and least understand.

They think it is docility.
