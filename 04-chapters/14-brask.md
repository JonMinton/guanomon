# Brask

---

> *Set down at the request of the Hummers' Guild, for its archive, in the thirty-first year of the keeping.*

The voice was the easy part. Everyone has that backwards, so I will say it first. People treat the carrying of a voice as the marvel — the seventh-bell broadcast, the woman with the hat, the whole warm nightly business — and treat what came after as a footnote. It is the other way round. The voice I solved in a season, and most of that was luck: I asked one question the others were too busy grieving to ask, and it worked. After that it was engineering. Engineering is the part worth talking about. No one ever wants to talk about it. I do not understand that. I have stopped expecting it to change.

A sympathetic resonance is a simple thing. Strike a fork, and a second fork tuned to its pitch will answer across a room, untouched. We knew it as boys. It was a party trick. Carrying a voice was only that trick made faithful enough to hold speech. But a fork that answers is a fork that has been given an instruction, if you are willing to see it that way, and almost no one was, because they were all still thinking about *talking*. I was not interested in talking. I wanted to know whether a thing could be made to *do* — to move, to turn, to hold a course — on nothing but a pitch sent to it across the empty air. That is a better question. It took years. I liked all of them.

To make a thing move on its own it must carry its own force, and force is not my art. I do resonance. I do not do fuel. I have never pretended to, which is already more than most of my guild manage; most of them will nod at a thing rather than admit there is an edge to what they know. I have tested this. It holds. Fuel is the dwarves' art. Here is the one part of this account that is about a person and not a problem. I put it in because it is true, not because it is meant to move anyone: I could not have done any of it without Korrin.

Korrin is a fuel engineer, and the only man I have worked with who did not waste my time. We are alike, I am told — I do not know enough about people to judge it, but I will grant that we want the same thing, which is for the work to be good, and that neither of us cares much for anything else other men seem to think matters. He does not mind that I am a mage and I do not mind that he is a dwarf. We worked out early, both of us a little relieved, that neither of us had ever understood why a person was supposed to care about that. He brought me the dial — the way the same dried muck gives a long slow heat or a single blow, depending only on how it is packed and pressed — and I brought him the fork, and between us we asked the question that mattered. *What if the blow could be steered?*

I will not spare you much of this, because it is the best part. The first problem is weight. A singing-engine small enough to ride in the breast of a flying thing has almost no voice; its answer to the controlling fork is faint, and faint is fatal, because a device that mishears its instruction by a hair at the launch will miss by a field at the distance. So you make the engine louder, which makes it heavier, which needs more fuel, which makes it heavier again — every engineer knows that spiral and hates it — or you make the *listening* finer, so that a faint clear pitch is enough. We chose listening. Korrin tuned the burn so steady that the device's own running did not blur its hearing; I cut the engine down and down until it was barely a single reed; and we married the two, and the thing held its course clean across a yard on a pitch I could have hummed under my breath. Korrin looked at it and said it was clean. It was. The cleanest thing I have ever built.

After that the problems multiply, which is pleasant. One device is a curiosity. The difficulty and the beauty are in *many*. You cannot steer a hundred by hand, so you set them on a graded ladder of pitches and let a few controlling forks govern the whole flight — and the flight holds together not because one hand steers each device, but because each is listening to the same few tones, and to the devices nearest it on the ladder. Out of that, with almost no one directing it, a large coordinated motion comes. I think it the best result of my life. I have never once got a single person to care about it. They only want to know what it is *for*.

What it is for is not my department, and the question bores me a little, but I will answer it, since it is the only thing anyone ever asks. They tell me it will change how the lands across the water are managed, and how order is kept at home, and I expect they are right; Korrin's people and the new fellowships have plans for it I have not bothered to follow. Someone asked me once, idle, over the work, whether one could be made large enough — or made to swarm thick enough — to bring down something *truly* large. They meant the source, I think. The big beasts in the deep mountains. Should one ever turn troublesome, they said. Or need correcting. I told them yes, of course; it is only a question of mass and number; the principle does not care how large the target is. They seemed unsettled by how fast I answered. I never understood that. It was an easy question. The hard questions are all in the listening.

I came up under Vesser. People are sometimes surprised by that, because they think of him as he is now — a name on the better invitations, a man who can fill a hall by agreeing to stand in it, the great genial face of everything the Hum has *done for us all* — and they think of me, when they think of me at all, as something that happened in a back room. But when I was young he had the bench and the standing and I had neither, and I will say plainly that he gave me my start. He simply could never sit still long enough to use his own. He was not a details man. A problem would be three moves from solved and he would be gone — off to a gathering, a delegation come to be charmed, some question of who had to be told what and in what order. By every account he loved the *meaning* of the work more than the work; I am told he is half the reason the country took the Hum to heart as fast as it did, that he made it feel like a wonder instead of a wire. Perhaps. You cannot solve *fashionable*. It was never a problem I could hold. So while he was out making the thing beloved, I stayed at the bench and made the next thing, and then the thing after that, and if he noticed that the boy he had handed his spare problems to had ended up building everything that came after, he never said so to me. I do not hold it against him. He gave me a quiet bench and went off to be adored. I had the better end of that, by a distance neither of us has ever measured the same way.

For the record, since this is meant to be a record: the voice-carrying was mine, and the steering is mine. You will not find my name on either in anything they hum to the provinces. The Guild prefers to say the understanding was *the guilds'*. Vesser's name comes up, and the woman's, and it is all correct enough in its way and I do not argue it. A name is not a problem. I have never once managed to make myself mind. I did the work. The work does not ask that anyone know I did it. It asks to be done well. It was.

They are calling the small ones hummingbirds now, which is a soft word for a clean piece of work, but I have stopped minding what people call things. The name is not the problem. The next problem is the problem. Korrin thinks we can halve the weight again, and I think he is right; I think that, if we are careful, we will build a thing that hears one voice across the whole width of the kingdom and goes, dead straight, to the single place it is told. I would like to see it done before I die. It would be very clean.

That is the work. I am told an account like this is supposed to have more people in it. There were never many people in it, for me. There was the fork, and there was the fuel, and there was Korrin, who understood, and there was the listening — the only thing I have ever loved, and the thing that is about to become the loudest in the world.
