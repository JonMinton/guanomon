# Dalla — Private

---

The hat is in a cupboard. I should say that, since I made it funny for them and never said where it went. It is in a cupboard, in the dark, shaped like the ledge I fell off, and once a year I take it out and look at it and put it back, and I have never decided whether that is the saddest thing I do or the most sensible. A little of both, probably. Most things I do are a little of both. That is what the years give you, if they give you anything: the ability to hold the joke and the cost in the same hand and not drop either.

I am very good on air. I want to say that to no one, here, where it costs nothing to say. I am *good* at it. People think the persona is a cage they put me in and I climb the bars all day. It is not a cage. It is a craft. I have spent thirty years learning to deliver one true sentence — *I fell* — in a tone so warm and so quick that a roomful of people will laugh and a nation will hear it and not one of them, not one, will catch that I have just told them the entire terrible truth and they have applauded me for my modesty. That is not nothing. A worse woman would have been destroyed by what happened to me. I turned it into an *act*, and the act is genuinely skilled, and I am, on my better days, almost proud of it. There. I have said a proud thing in the dark. It will not survive contact with the morning but it was true when I said it.

What they did was so simple. They did not silence me. That is the part nobody understands, the part I could not make them understand even if I tried, which I never will. I was not gagged. I was given a *show*. I was handed a chair and a kind man and a warm room and told: say whatever you like, dear, say the truest thing you know, say *I fell, I am nothing, you have named your machine after my worst afternoon* — say all of it, and watch. And I said it, and watched, and they laughed, and the laughing closed over the truth like water over a stone, and the official story — *she discovered it* — sailed on top of my own honest denial without so much as getting its feet wet. They didn't need to lie. They had me to tell the truth for them, charmingly, into a device that turns truth into weather.

Sennet is the worst of it because he is the kindest of it. *I look,* he says. He doesn't. He couldn't bear to; there's no room in him to put me; he poured himself into the listening face so long ago that there's nothing behind it now to do any actual listening, and I love him, in the practised way you love the person you have been pretending *with* for a decade, and I watch him and I think: *that is the far end of this road, and you are further along it than I am, and you cannot see the road at all any more, only the next guest.* I keep something private precisely so I will not become him. This. The cupboard. The hat. The dark. The fact that there is still a *me* in here that the warm voice is *about* and not simply made of. He let his go. I keep mine in a cupboard with the hat.

I think, sometimes, about the women who stayed. The shovellers. We started in the same dirt, on the same crews, before any of this, going down for the gold and stepping past the muck like everyone always had — and I slipped, and they didn't, and that single afternoon sorted us forever. They went on shovelling, every day, for twenty years, in the actual filth, while I went on being *a unit*, very clean, very famous, drawn and paid for and never thought of as a person. I expect they have a flat opinion of me. They'd be right to. I fell in it *once.* I have dined out on one fall for thirty years. They have been in it every day since and not one of them has a hat. If I ever met one I think I would not be able to look at her, and I think she would look at me for exactly as long as it took to decide I wasn't worth the trouble, and then she would go back to her drink, and she would be *right*, and it would be the most honest exchange of my entire public life.

The joke I do about the money is that I get nothing. *You were charged for me; I get nothing* — it brings the house down, and it is not true, and the untruth is the whole craft of it: a woman with nothing is pitiable, and pity is death on air, so I hand them a woman *robbed*, which is funny, in place of a woman *paid*, which is not. I am paid. I am paid very well. I have more than I will spend and a house I rattle around in and people to keep it warm. I am also fairly sure, on the evenings I let myself count, that *very well* is a long way short of what I am worth — that somewhere there is a ledger where every Dalla drawn into every house in the country is totted up, and the figure at the foot of it would stop my breath, and I am paid a wage and they keep the ledger, and the gap between the two is simply the arrangement. But here is the thing I cannot make even myself take seriously past midnight: I do not really mind about the money. The money is not the wound.

The wound is that I am *worked*. Someone decided, years ago, what I am for, and I have performed it so long and so well and so comfortably that I can no longer find the seam where they stop and I start. And the genius of it — I do them the credit; it is genius — is that there is nothing to push against. I am not kept anywhere I would not choose to stay. The room is warm. The work is easy now, easier than the shovel by a margin I am ashamed to measure. Every door out of this leads, plainly and at once, *down* — to less warmth, less ease, less of the one thing I have ever been good at — and so I stay, and I keep quiet, and I tell the joke about the money, and on the bad nights I understand that the door is open, that it has stood open for years, and that I am the one who shuts it, nightly, from the inside. It's warmer that way. That's the joke I can't tell.

There is a dragon, somewhere under all of us, that the whole thing runs on. I think about her too, though I have no reason to and have never seen her. A body they feed and keep and reinforce and schedule, valued exactly as long as it stays in its position and does its one thing, watched all day by people who are quite certain they own her. I don't know why she's in my head. We have nothing in common. She is a beast and I am a national treasure. I am sure we have nothing in common. I take the hat out, once a year, and I look at it, and I put it back, and somewhere far below me, in the dark, something enormous that nobody has ever asked a single question of goes on quietly being the thing that everything is built on, and is watched, and is owned, and says nothing, and waits.

Good night. To no one. Good night.
