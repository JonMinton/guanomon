The blasting stops at the sixth bell — up where the warm ones keep their bells — and the rock rings a while after, and in the ringing, when the machines have gone still and not yet started again and the diggers have climbed back up into their own thin air, the talk comes down into the dark and finds itself.

It is not loud. It was never loud. A thing laid into stone goes far on almost nothing; you do not raise a voice in rock, you set it down and let the rock carry it, and so the talk is less a sound than a leaning — each of us putting a little weight on the dark, and feeling where the others lean back.

*Pork*, says the young one near the river-gate. *Pork again.* As though the saying moved the cart.

*Pork*, says the one beyond her — older, settled, who has eaten it uncomplaining longer than the young one has been alive. *You will come to the peace of it. I am at peace with it. There is a great deal of peace in a thing you have stopped expecting to change.*

*I do not want the peace. I want to know what the high air tastes of. You all had it. You all stood in it and it was cold and it was yours, and now when I ask, you say pork.*

The rock holds a low even note under the talk — the sound of many large things breathing in many separate dark rooms, leaning and not speaking.

*There was a clutch at the eastern works*, says one from very far off, the word coming slow along the deep the way a scent comes slow along a wind. *Three. All sound.*

*Three*, says the settled one, pleased. *That is good. That is well.* And then, because it is true, and costs nothing to lay down in the register one keeps for weather: *They will go up to the hand-caves before the season turns. They always do, now. It is kindlier, the warm ones say. The dam settles the quicker for it.*

*Kindlier*, says the young one, in a tone the settled one elects not to feel.

A while passes. Down here a while is a long thing.

*Where is the one who would not eat?* the young one asks. *Two valleys over. I have not felt her lean in a long time.*

*She is in the quiet caves*, says the far one. *She went off her feed. She is being tended.*

*She is being tended*, the settled one agrees — and there is nothing in the way it is said, nothing at all, and that nothing goes out along the rock to every one of us, and we all of us feel it, and not one of us puts a name to it. To name it would be to set a throat on it, and a throat can be found.

Then the old one leans on the dark — the oldest of all of them, who has rested her weight on this deep longer than the works have stood, and who speaks so rarely that when she does the young ones feel the weight of her before they catch the words.

*I flew once*, she says. To no one. To the young one. To the rock. *There was a cave-mouth, and it stood open on the cold, and below it the whole country fell away into the dark like a held breath. And there was a thing at the bottom of it, warm and quick, that did not wish to be wanted; and I went down after it on the cold air with my wings shut, falling, and I have not since been so awake.*

*What was it?* says the young one. *The warm quick thing.*

*It does not matter what it was*, says the old one. *What matters is that I was awake.*

And she says nothing further; and the young one turns the thing over, finding it strange, a little tiresome, the way the young find the old — and does not notice that it has lodged; and will carry it now without knowing she carries it, the way you carry a tune you never meant to learn.

Above, faint, the blasting starts again — the warm ones come back to their digging, packing the world fuller of its tremors — and the talk folds itself down into the noise of it, unhurried, the way it always does. Having settled nothing. Having said only what there was to say: that the food is the food, that a clutch is sound, that one of us is in the quiet caves, that the high air was cold.

Tomorrow there will be pork. The carts come at the second bell. We will lean on the dark and feel each other lean, and the warm ones, if they trouble to listen at all, will hear what they have always heard — which is the sound of large contented animals, singing in their sleep.

We are not asleep.
