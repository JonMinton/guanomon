# Grundrak

---

Measure a thing and it stops lying to you. That is my whole trade, and my whole crime, depending who you ask, and I have long since stopped minding which.

I set this down early, you should know — while the stuff is still a curiosity on my bench and not yet the wage that lights every house and is charged back to it; before the chair; before the towns go bright of an evening. The afternoon the old world ended has already happened. The old world has merely not been told. It seems I am the one telling it.

Start with what I am, since the people upstairs spent three centuries arranging not to look at it. I am a dwarf: a little over five feet standing, near five across the shoulders, four through from breastbone to spine. A brick. The word has been thrown at me for an insult and I have kept it — kept it gladly, in the end, because a brick is an honest object and I would sooner be an honest object than a pretty one. A brick is what you build the load-bearing wall from. Stand enough of us in a course and you have the foundation the whole bright world sets its feet on and never thinks of — the mined ore, the cut stone, the shored roof, the deep kept dry, century on century, while the men above sang about steel and the mages above sold them wonders, and not one song in all that singing was ever about the wall that held the hall up. I did not invent that grievance. I inherited it, the way you inherit a trade, and I have put it to work, which is more than the songmakers ever did with theirs.

So. The assay. You bring me a substance and I do not ask what it is for, or whose it is, or what the songs say of it. I ask what is in it, and what it will do under load, at heat, in quantity. That is the whole of the procedure. The mages asked what a thing *meant*; I ask what it will *do* — and an arcane art, once you get up close to it with a scale and a flame, is mostly a plain trick with a rule built round it forbidding anyone else to learn the trick. Take the rule away and you are left with an ordinary man and an ordinary craft and a great deal of wasted reverence. That is what an assay does to a mage, and it was bound to make me enemies, and I found I could carry that.

How it came to me was an accident, and I will set it down straight, because the tellings that come after will be prettier and wronger — they will want a saint in them, and the world has always preferred a saint to a sum. A collection crew came up from the deep, filthy with the stuff nobody had yet learned to want, the way crews came up in those years: down for the gold, and back disgusted with everything that was not gold. The woman who led them stripped to the skin by the fire, the way you do when you have been in the deep and cannot bear your own clothes another minute. Her clothes went near the flame to dry. The day's muck was dried into the weave of them — and so, though it took us a long while to grasp it, was the honey, ground in from the bee-side of the work along with all the rest.

It did not burn. It *detonated*. It took the fire and a good part of the workshop with it, and it said, in one flat shout of force, that the filth the whole world had spent three hundred years stepping over to reach the gold was the most concentrated thing any of us had ever held in a hand. The old valuers had it exactly half right — gold the treasure, this the filth. They had simply kept the wrong half.

That is the accident. *Where there's muck there's brass*, the old people used to say, and they meant it small — a saying for getting by, for making do. They did not know they were naming the foundation of the world. But an accident is not a discovery. An accident is a question shouted once and never repeated. I made it repeat.

I brought the stuff to the bench and I asked it the only questions I know how to ask. Wet, it does nothing: it is muck, it drinks your flame and ruins your boots, and there is a mercy in that, or the pits would have gone up generations ago. *Dried*, it will burn. Dried *and* met with the catalyst — the honey, abundant and mundane and in everything — it does not burn, it *answers*, all at once. And I found the dial between the two: how the packing and the pressing decide whether you draw a long honest heat to turn a wheel through the night, or a single blow to take down a wall. This muck shines brighter than the sun and hits harder than any metal, and it does either on command, once it has been asked properly. I asked it properly. That is the assay, and the assay is the only honest part.

So I proposed the trials, and I proposed the terms, and the terms are the part they will never forgive me, because the terms were fair.

Hear what I gave them. I did not ask the mages to debase themselves at some task chosen to humiliate. I let them name the work. They named the stamps — the great iron heads that lift and fall and crush ore to meal — because the stamps are honest labour made visible, and because lifting is the oldest thing their art can do and the proudest. Good. Lift them, then. We will count the strikes, and we will count them past the first hour, past the second, into the night and out the other side, and whoever's stamps are still falling at the dawn count has proved the only thing a power is ever asked to prove, which is *can you do it again, and again, and again, without rest, without a temper, without a soul that gets tired.*

Vesser was magnificent in the first hour. I will say that here and I will say it anywhere. I have watched a great many men be good at things and I have rarely watched anyone be *that* good at anything. The heads went up like he was breathing them up, clean and high and ringing, and the clerk's count ran ahead of mine, and the crowd made the noise crowds make when they think they are watching the old world win. I did not make that noise. I was watching the curve. I have spent my life watching curves, and I knew the shape of this one before the man making it did. Brilliance has a slope. It is the slope of a thing spending itself.

By the third hour his strikes had a hitch in them, the smallest catch, a man's wrist remembering it is a wrist. By the fifth there was sweat standing on him in the cold, and his count had stopped running ahead and started keeping pace, and then it stopped keeping pace. My engine did not know any of this was happening. It does not know it is in a contest. It eats the dried fuel and turns the wheel and the heads come down at the rhythm I set them, the same in the dark hour as the bright one, the same when Vesser was a legend and the same when he was a sixty-year-old man in a wet shirt who could not lift his arms.

I called the halt myself, before the dawn count. I want that recorded. I sent a man with a stool and a cup of water and I stopped the clock while he sat, and I have been told since that this was the cruelty — that the water was the worst of it, that to be *spared* in front of them all was a thing he could not have survived being. Perhaps. I am a poor judge of what a courtesy costs a proud man, having spent my youth on the receiving end of theirs. We were *graciously permitted*, my people, for a great many centuries. We were *kindly tolerated* below. I learned what kindness from above is worth, and I will not apologise for offering some back and meaning it, even if it killed him slower than contempt would have.

This is the part the histories get wrong, so I will set it down plainly. It was not a triumph. I had thought it would be. I had carried the picture of it for years — the floor, the count, the curve crossing, the old order watching its own arithmetic on the wall — and when it came it lasted about as long as the count itself and then it began, very quietly, to go out of me, and it has been going out of me ever since.

Because here is the thing I did not assay, and I assay everything. I won the floor in order to make the floor not matter. I broke the mages' monopoly so that no man's birth or guild or art would decide what his labour was worth — only the work, only the curve, only the honest fact of the strikes. And the world looked at what I had done and it did not stop minding birth and guild and art. It simply added a new thing to mind, and put me at the top of it, and called *that* justice.

They will give me a throne for this, in time. I can feel it coming the way I felt the curve coming. And I will take it, because the only thing worse than sitting in that chair would be leaving it for one of them. And the day I sit in it I will have proved, with my whole life, the one proposition I most wanted to disprove:

that the chair was real all along, and that even I would climb over the gold to reach it.

So the trials are over, and found — let the histories dress it however they like — in favour of muck over magic. The curve crossed. The old power sat down on a stool and drank its water, and there is nothing left to prove in a yard with stamps and a clock.

What remains is to show the rest of them what the curve means. The neighbours across the water who still burn wood and field their brave ranked lines of men. The walls that still believe they are arguments. The trials settled what the stuff *is*. Now we settle what it *does* — at distance, in quantity, to anyone who has not yet had it explained.

Now onto the demonstrations.
